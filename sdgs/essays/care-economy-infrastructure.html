The Care Economy Is Infrastructure

照护经济就是基础设施

SDG 5 will remain rhetorical until care is counted, paid and publicly financed

C1 · 雅思 7.5+ / 托福 100+8 min readSDG 5 · SDG 8 · SDG 1

SDG 5 will remain rhetorical until care is counted, paid and publicly financed.

The invisible subsidy

Every economy runs on care. Someone feeds children, tends the sick, supports the elderly and maintains the households that make paid work possible. Most of this work is unpaid, and most of it is done by women. The ILO estimates that women perform roughly three times as much unpaid care work as men; globally, this amounts to billions of hours every day.

Because it is unpaid, care does not appear in GDP as a productive contribution. Because it is invisible, it is treated as a private responsibility rather than public infrastructure. The result is a hidden subsidy: households, and disproportionately women, absorb the costs of reproducing the labour force, while fiscal policy behaves as if that subsidy were free.

SDG 5 recognises unpaid care through target 5.4, which calls for the recognition and valuation of unpaid care and domestic work, and for shared responsibility within households. But recognition without resources is unlikely to change the distribution.

Why inclusion without redistribution fails

A common policy story says that increasing female labour force participation will promote growth and empowerment. The story is incomplete. If women enter paid work while continuing to perform most unpaid care, they face a double shift: longer total working hours, greater stress and a persistent wage penalty.

In many countries, the cost of childcare exceeds the expected earnings of low-wage mothers. Where public services are absent, women rely on informal arrangements, usually with other women—grandmothers, neighbours, domestic workers—who are themselves poorly paid and unprotected.

This is why care policy is not a side issue for gender equality. It determines whether paid work is genuinely accessible, whether women can choose whether to have children, and whether the burden of care is shared with men and with the state.

The care economy as a multiplier

Investing in care is often portrayed as consumption. In fact, it is investment. Childcare, early education, elder care and health services create jobs, support labour supply and improve outcomes for children and older people. The care sector is labour-intensive, with relatively high employment multipliers, and much of the spending stays in the local economy.

Public investment in care can also reduce the fiscal cost of later interventions. Early childhood development improves educational attainment and lifetime earnings; accessible elder care reduces hospitalisation and informal coping costs. These returns are difficult to measure in narrow budget cycles, which is precisely why they are systematically underprovided.

From recognition to redistribution

First, governments should establish universal or affordable childcare and early education, with public financing and decent pay for care workers. The Nordic model is often cited, but the principle is more important than the template: care must be treated as a public service, not a private market for the affluent.

Second, paid parental leave should be available to both parents, with incentives for fathers to take it. Leave that is formally gender-neutral but culturally reserved for mothers will not change the division of labour.

Third, care workers—domestic workers, home-care aides, early childhood educators—need labour rights, living wages and social protection. The care economy is not only about the people receiving care; it is also about the quality of jobs created.

Fourth, gender-responsive budgeting should make care visible in fiscal decisions. Governments can publish time-use data, estimate the value of unpaid care and assess how budget cuts affect women disproportionately. Austerity is not gender-neutral: when public services are cut, unpaid work expands to fill the gap.

The politics of time

Ultimately, the care agenda is about time and power. It challenges the assumption that productivity only counts when it is exchanged for money, and that families—rather than societies—should absorb the costs of dependency and reproduction.

SDG 5 will not be achieved by exhortation. It requires public money, legal rights and a cultural shift in the division of labour. The question is whether governments are willing to treat care as infrastructure: expensive, essential and worthy of long-term investment.

A society that cannot count care will not be able to value it. And a society that does not value care will continue to assign its costs to those with the least power to refuse them.

中文译文 · CHINESE

照护经济就是基础设施

每个经济都依赖照护。有人喂养孩子、照顾病人、支持老人、维持让有偿工作成为可能的家庭。这些工作大多无酬，且大多由女性承担。国际劳工组织估计，女性承担的无酬照护约为男性的三倍；全球每天累计数十亿小时。因为无酬，它不以生产性贡献进入 GDP；因为不可见，它被当作家庭私事而非公共基础设施。结果是隐性补贴：家庭、尤其是女性，吸收劳动力再生产的成本，而财政政策却把这项补贴当作免费。

一种常见的政策叙事说，提高女性劳动参与率会促进增长与赋权。这个叙事不完整。如果女性进入有偿工作却继续承担大部分无酬照护，她们面对的是双重负担：总工时更长、压力更大、工资惩罚持续。在许多国家，托育成本超过低薪母亲的预期收入；公共服务缺位时，女性依赖非正式安排，通常由其他女性——祖母、邻居、家政工——承担，而这些女性本身报酬低、缺乏保护。

照护投资常被当作消费，实际上是投资。托育、早期教育、养老与健康服务创造就业、支持劳动供给、改善儿童与老人结局。照护部门劳动密集、就业乘数较高，支出多留在本地经济。公共照护投资还能降低后期干预的财政成本：早期发展改善教育与终身收入，可及的养老服务减少住院与非正式应对成本。这些回报难以在短预算周期中测量，因此被系统性供给不足。

从承认走向再分配，需要：建立普惠或可负担的托育与早期教育，公共出资并保障照护工作者体面薪酬；为父母双方提供带薪假，并激励父亲使用；赋予家政工、居家照护者、幼教工作者劳动权利、生活工资与社会保护；推行性别响应型预算，公布时间使用数据、估算无酬照护价值，评估削减预算对女性的不对称影响。紧缩不是性别中立的：公共服务退却时，无酬工作扩张填补缺口。

归根结底，照护议程关乎时间与权力。它挑战“只有进入市场交换才算生产力”的假设，也挑战“家庭而非社会应吸收依赖与再生产成本”的假设。SDG 5 不会靠劝诫实现，它需要公共资金、法律权利与劳动分工的文化转变。一个不会计算照护的社会，也无法珍视照护；一个不珍视照护的社会，会继续把成本分配给最没有能力拒绝的人。
