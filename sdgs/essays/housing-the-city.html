Housing the City: SDG 11 and the Politics of Land

为城市提供住房：SDG 11 与土地政治

Affordable housing is not a residual of growth; it is a precondition for sustainable cities

C1 · 雅思 7.5+ / 托福 100+8 min readSDG 11 · SDG 1 · SDG 10

Affordable housing is not a residual of growth; it is a precondition for sustainable cities.

The urban century

More than half of the world's population now lives in cities, and the share is rising. Cities generate a large share of GDP and energy-related emissions. They are also where inequality becomes visible: in the distance between gated communities and informal settlements, in the commute times of those who cannot afford to live near work.

SDG 11 calls for inclusive, safe, resilient and sustainable cities. Its targets include adequate and affordable housing, basic services, public transport and participatory urban planning. These are not separate goals; they are interdependent.

Housing is the hinge. A home determines access to education, health care, employment and political membership. When housing is unaffordable, the city becomes a machine for sorting people by income.

The financialisation of shelter

In many cities, housing has been transformed from a place to live into an asset class. Global capital flows into real estate, buying up rental housing and driving up prices. In some markets, institutional investors own large portfolios of single-family homes; in others, luxury developments sit empty while waiting for returns.

Financialisation is not simply a matter of greedy landlords. It is structured by tax policy, planning rules, credit markets and the belief that property ownership should be a primary vehicle for household wealth. When housing is expected to appreciate faster than wages, affordability declines even if supply increases.

The result is a paradox: cities can have vacant units and homeless people at the same time. The problem is not only the number of houses; it is who can access them on what terms.

Informality and the right to the city

Around 1.1 billion people live in informal settlements, often without secure tenure, basic services or protection from eviction. These settlements are not anomalies; they are the product of failed housing policies, restrictive planning and unequal land markets.

Upgrading informal settlements requires more than infrastructure. It requires legal recognition, secure tenure, participatory planning and investment in public space. Evictions that displace communities in the name of redevelopment often destroy social networks and livelihoods.

The “right to the city” is not a slogan about individual property; it is a claim that urban inhabitants should have a voice in decisions that shape their lives. Participation is not a luxury of wealthy neighbourhoods.

Local finance and the politics of land

Cities cannot solve housing crises without fiscal capacity. Many local governments rely on property taxes, transfers and borrowing, but property tax collection is often weak and regressive. Land value capture—taxing the increase in land value created by public investment—can fund infrastructure and social housing without discouraging construction.

Public land is a strategic asset. Governments can use it for social housing, mixed-income developments and public services, rather than selling it to the highest bidder. Inclusionary zoning, which requires developers to include affordable units, can work if it is enforced and if the requirements are not passed on entirely to renters.

Rent regulation is contested. Well-designed rent stabilisation can protect tenants from sudden increases, but it can also reduce investment if not paired with supply policies. The lesson is not that regulation is good or bad in the abstract; it is that housing policy must be coherent across tax, planning, finance and social protection.

Building the inclusive city

A credible SDG 11 strategy would combine social housing investment, tenant protections, land value capture, participatory planning and climate-resilient construction. It would also address the root causes of housing demand: wage stagnation, unequal access to credit and the treatment of housing as a speculative asset.

The climate dimension is inseparable. Cities account for a large share of energy-related emissions, and poor housing is often the most vulnerable to heat, flooding and air pollution. Retrofitting existing buildings can reduce emissions and improve living conditions, but it can also raise rents if not accompanied by protections.

The city is not a natural phenomenon. It is a political project. Whether it becomes inclusive or exclusionary depends on decisions about land, money and voice. SDG 11 is an invitation to make those decisions democratically—and to recognise that housing is infrastructure, not a luxury.

中文译文 · CHINESE

为城市提供住房：SDG 11 与土地政治

世界一半以上人口居住在城市，且比例仍在上升。城市创造大量 GDP 与能源相关排放，也让不平等变得可见：封闭社区与非正规住区之间的距离，负担不起近工作地点住房者的通勤时间。SDG 11 要求建设包容、安全、有韧性、可持续的城市，其具体目标包括适足可负担住房、基本服务、公共交通与参与式城市规划；这些不是彼此分离的目标，而是相互依存。住房是枢纽：家决定教育、医疗、就业与政治成员资格；当住房不可负担，城市就变成按收入排序的机器。

在许多城市，住房已从居住场所变成资产类别。全球资本流入房地产，购买租赁住房、推高价格；一些市场机构投资者持有大量独栋住宅，另一些市场豪华开发空置等待回报。金融化不只是贪婪房东的问题，它由税收政策、规划规则、信贷市场与“房产应是家庭财富主要载体”的信念共同塑造。当住房预期升值快于工资，即使供给增加，可负担性也会下降。结果是悖论：城市可以同时存在空置住房与无家可归者。问题不只是房子数量，而是谁能以什么条件获得住房。

约 11 亿人生活在非正规住区，往往缺乏安全权属、基本服务或免于驱逐的保护。这些住区不是异常，而是住房政策失败、限制性规划与不平等土地市场的产物。改造非正规住区不只是基础设施，还需要法律承认、权属保障、参与式规划与公共空间投资。以再开发为名驱逐社区，往往摧毁社会网络与生计。“城市权利”不是个人产权的口号，而是城市居民应在其生活决策中有发言权的主张；参与不是富裕街区的奢侈品。

没有财政能力，城市无法解决住房危机。许多地方政府依赖房产税、转移支付与借贷，但房产税征收往往薄弱且累退。土地增值回收——对公共投资创造的土地增值征税——可以为基础设施与社会住房融资，而不抑制建设。公共土地是战略资产：政府可以用于社会住房、混合收入开发与公共服务，而不是卖给最高出价者。包容性区划要求开发商配建可负担单元，若执行有力且要求不完全转嫁给租户，可以奏效。租金管制有争议：设计良好的租金稳定可以保护租户免受突然上涨，但若缺乏供给政策配套，也可能减少投资。教训不是管制本身好坏，而是住房政策必须在税收、规划、金融与社会保护之间保持连贯。

可信的 SDG 11 战略应结合社会住房投资、租户保护、土地增值回收、参与式规划与气候韧性建设，也要处理住房需求的根源：工资停滞、信贷不平等与把住房当作投机资产。气候维度不可分割：城市占能源相关排放很大份额，差住房最易受热浪、洪水与空气污染影响；既有建筑改造可以减排并改善居住条件，但若缺乏保护，也可能推高租金。城市不是自然现象，而是政治项目。它变得包容还是排斥，取决于关于土地、金钱与发言权的决定。SDG 11 邀请我们民主地做这些决定，并承认住房是基础设施，不是奢侈品。
