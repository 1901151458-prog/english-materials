The Ocean Is a Commons, Not a Dumping Ground

海洋是公地，不是垃圾场

SDG 14 depends on enforceable commons rules, not voluntary pledges

C1–C2 · 雅思 7.5+ / 托福 105+8 min readSDG 14 · SDG 13 · SDG 12

SDG 14 depends on enforceable commons rules, not voluntary pledges.

The ocean as infrastructure

The ocean is not a backdrop to the climate crisis; it is part of the planetary machinery. It absorbs around ninety per cent of the excess heat trapped by greenhouse gases and a significant share of anthropogenic carbon dioxide. It regulates weather, supports biodiversity and sustains the livelihoods of hundreds of millions of people.

Yet the ocean is also a commons without a sovereign. The high seas cover nearly half the planet and lie beyond national jurisdiction. Fishing, shipping, deep-sea mining, scientific research and carbon storage all compete for space and resources under a patchwork of rules.

SDG 14 calls for conserving and sustainably using the oceans, seas and marine resources. The challenge is not a lack of targets; it is a lack of enforceable institutions.

The BBNJ breakthrough

In 2023, states adopted the Biodiversity Beyond National Jurisdiction agreement, known as the BBNJ treaty. It creates a framework for marine protected areas, environmental impact assessments, marine genetic resources and capacity-building on the high seas.

The agreement is significant because it moves beyond the voluntary language that has dominated ocean governance. It establishes institutions and procedures, including a conference of the parties and a scientific body. But its effectiveness depends on ratification and implementation. A treaty that is not ratified, or that lacks financing, is only a promise.

The BBNJ also raises questions of equity. Marine genetic resources may have commercial value, and developing countries want a fair share of benefits. Capacity-building and technology transfer are not side payments; they are conditions for a legitimate regime.

The plastics crisis

Plastic pollution is one of the most visible failures of the current system. An estimated eleven million tonnes of plastic enter the ocean each year. Most of it comes from land-based sources, and a significant share is generated by a small number of countries and companies.

Negotiations for a global plastics treaty have exposed deep divisions. Some states want binding caps on production; others prefer waste management and recycling. The petrochemical industry has lobbied against production limits. A treaty that focuses only on downstream waste will not solve the problem; it may even legitimise continued expansion of plastic production.

A credible agreement would combine production reduction, design standards, extended producer responsibility and finance for waste management in developing countries. It would also need to address the informal waste pickers who do much of the recycling work under hazardous conditions.

Fisheries, subsidies and justice

Fisheries subsidies are a classic commons problem. Governments spend billions of dollars annually on fuel subsidies, vessel construction and access agreements that contribute to overcapacity and overfishing. The World Trade Organization reached an agreement on harmful fisheries subsidies in 2022, but it covers only a subset of subsidies and has not yet entered into force for all members.

Illegal, unreported and unregulated fishing undermines rules and steals revenue from coastal states. Monitoring, control and surveillance require technology, patrol capacity and regional cooperation. Small island developing states are particularly vulnerable: they control vast ocean areas relative to their land size but lack the resources to monitor them.

Blue finance is often proposed as a solution. Yet financial mechanisms must be designed to support small-scale fishers, not only large investors. Debt-for-nature swaps, blue bonds and blended finance can mobilise capital, but they can also create new forms of conditionality and debt.

From pledges to rules

The ocean agenda has no shortage of summits, declarations and voluntary commitments. What it lacks is implementation. A credible SDG 14 strategy would prioritise ratification and financing of the BBNJ, a binding plastics treaty with production limits, reform of fisheries subsidies, and support for community-led marine management.

It would also require a shift in how we value the ocean. The ocean is not only a resource to be extracted; it is a system that makes life on Earth possible. Treating it as a commons means accepting limits, sharing benefits and holding powerful actors accountable.

The ocean is often described as the last frontier. In political terms, it is one of the first tests of whether humanity can govern a shared resource without a sovereign. The answer will shape not only SDG 14, but the credibility of multilateralism itself.

中文译文 · CHINESE

海洋是公地，不是垃圾场

海洋不是气候危机的背景，而是地球机器的一部分。它吸收约九成温室气体捕获的多余热量与相当份额的人为二氧化碳，调节天气、支撑生物多样性、维系数亿人生计。然而海洋也是没有主权者的公地：公海覆盖近半个地球，位于国家管辖之外；渔业、航运、深海采矿、科研与碳储存都在一套拼凑规则下争夺空间与资源。SDG 14 要求保护和可持续利用海洋资源，挑战不在目标缺失，而在可执行的制度缺失。

2023 年，各国通过《国家管辖范围以外区域海洋生物多样性协定》（BBNJ），为公海海洋保护区、环境影响评估、海洋遗传资源与能力建设建立框架。它重要，因为超越了主导海洋治理的自愿语言，建立了缔约方会议与科学机构等程序；但其有效性取决于批准与落实。未批准、缺资金的条约只是承诺。BBNJ 也提出公平问题：海洋遗传资源可能有商业价值，发展中国家要求公平分享收益；能力建设与技术转移不是旁支，而是合法性的条件。

塑料污染是当前体系最明显的失败之一。估计每年有 1100 万吨塑料进入海洋，大部分来自陆地，相当份额由少数国家与公司产生。全球塑料条约谈判暴露深刻分歧：一些国家要求对生产设定有约束力的上限，另一些偏好废弃物管理与回收；石化行业游说反对生产限制。仅关注下游废弃物的条约不会解决问题，甚至可能使塑料生产扩张合法化。可信协议应结合生产削减、设计标准、生产者延伸责任，以及为发展中国家废弃物管理提供资金；也必须正视在危险条件下完成大量回收工作的非正规拾荒者。

渔业补贴是典型的公地问题。政府每年花费数十亿美元用于燃料补贴、渔船建造与准入协议，助长产能过剩与过度捕捞。世贸组织 2022 年达成有害渔业补贴协议，但只覆盖部分补贴，且尚未对所有成员生效。非法、不报告和不受管制捕捞破坏规则、窃取沿海国收入；监测、控制与监视需要技术、巡逻能力与区域合作。小岛屿发展中国家尤其脆弱：它们控制的海洋面积相对陆地巨大，却缺乏监测资源。蓝色金融常被当作解决方案，但机制必须支持小规模渔民，而不只是大型投资者；债务换自然、蓝色债券与混合融资可以动员资本，也可能制造新的条件性与债务。

海洋议程不缺峰会、宣言与自愿承诺，缺的是执行。可信的 SDG 14 战略应优先批准并为 BBNJ 提供资金、达成含生产限制的有约束力塑料条约、改革渔业补贴、支持社区主导的海洋管理。它还需要改变我们评价海洋的方式：海洋不只是可提取的资源，而是让地球生命成为可能的系统。把海洋当作公地，意味着接受限制、分享收益、追究强势行为体责任。海洋常被称为最后的前沿；在政治上，它是人类能否在没有主权者的情况下治理共享资源的最早考验之一。答案不仅塑造 SDG 14，也塑造多边主义的可信度。
