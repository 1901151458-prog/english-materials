Decent Work in the Age of AI

人工智能时代的体面工作

Automation will not simply destroy jobs; it will redistribute bargaining power

C1 · 雅思 7.5+ / 托福 100+8 min readSDG 8 · SDG 10 · SDG 4

AI is less a job-killer than a bargaining-power reshuffler; SDG 8 requires governing the distribution of gains.

Beyond the automation anxiety

Every technological panic follows a familiar script: machines will replace workers, jobs will disappear, and societies must adapt. AI has intensified that script because it reaches cognitive tasks that were once considered safe. Yet the historical record suggests that the more important effects are rarely aggregate job destruction; they are changes in task composition, wage bargaining and the location of value capture.

A job is a bundle of tasks, relationships and rights. AI can automate some tasks without eliminating the job, and it can eliminate a job without automating all of its tasks. The distributional consequences depend on institutions: whether workers can negotiate over how technology is deployed, whether social protection is tied to employment, and whether the gains are taxed and shared.

The informal majority

Roughly two billion people work in the informal economy. For them, the AI transition is not primarily about remote work or productivity tools; it is about whether their livelihoods become more precarious. Informal workers often lack contracts, social insurance and collective representation, which makes them especially vulnerable to sudden shifts in demand and to algorithmic intermediation.

Platform work is the clearest example. Algorithms assign tasks, set prices, monitor performance and impose penalties. The workers may be classified as independent contractors, which allows platforms to externalise risk and avoid contributions to social protection. If AI makes this model more efficient, it may spread to new sectors without extending the rights that formal employment historically provided.

Productivity without bargaining power

The standard story says that AI raises productivity, and that higher productivity eventually raises wages. That transmission is not automatic. It depends on the relative bargaining power of labour, the degree of competition in product markets, and the rules that govern data and intellectual property.

When a handful of firms control the foundational models, compute and distribution channels, the gains from AI may accrue disproportionately to capital. Workers may experience intensification rather than liberation: more monitoring, faster pacing, and less discretion. The result can be a paradox of productivity growth alongside stagnant or falling labour income.

A policy agenda for just transition

First, social protection must be decoupled from the standard employment relationship. Universal or near-universal schemes—child benefits, health coverage, basic income support—can cushion transitions without trapping workers in bad jobs. Financing them requires a tax base that captures value created in the digital economy.

Second, algorithmic management needs democratic accountability. Workers should have rights to information, explanation and contestation when algorithms determine hiring, scheduling, pay or dismissal. Data protection and labour law must be updated together; privacy is also a labour issue.

Third, skills policy should move beyond short-term bootcamps. The goal is not merely to supply workers for the current demand; it is to build capabilities for adaptation, including foundational literacy, digital judgment and the ability to learn continuously. SDG 4's promise of lifelong learning is central here.

Fourth, collective bargaining and social dialogue must be part of technology governance. The countries that manage technological change best are not necessarily those with the most generous safety nets; they are those where workers, employers and governments negotiate the terms of deployment.

Global rules for a global technology

AI supply chains cross borders: data, compute, talent and intellectual property are globally distributed. Yet labour protections remain national, and platform companies can choose the jurisdiction with the weakest rules. This creates a race to the bottom that no single country can solve alone.

International cooperation should focus on minimum standards for algorithmic transparency, cross-border social security coordination for platform workers, and a fair share of tax revenue from digital activity. The UN's Global Digital Compact, agreed in 2024, provides a starting point, but its commitments are largely voluntary.

SDG 8 is not only about the quantity of jobs. It is about whether work allows people to live with dignity, exercise voice and share in the gains from productivity. If AI is governed only by market incentives, it may produce growth that is impressive in the aggregate and corrosive in the workplace.

中文译文 · CHINESE

人工智能时代的体面工作

每一次技术恐慌都遵循熟悉的剧本：机器取代工人，岗位消失，社会必须适应。人工智能让这个剧本更紧张，因为它触及了曾被认为安全的认识类任务。但历史经验表明，更重要的影响通常不是就业总量的毁灭，而是任务构成、工资议价与价值捕获位置的变化。

一份工作是一组任务、关系与权利的集合。AI 可以自动化部分任务而不消灭岗位，也可以消灭岗位而不自动化全部任务。分配后果取决于制度：工人能否就技术部署进行谈判，社会保护是否与就业绑定，收益是否被征税并分享。

约二十亿人在非正规经济中工作。对他们而言，AI 转型不是远程办公或生产力工具的问题，而是生计是否变得更加脆弱。非正规劳动者往往没有合同、社会保险与集体代表，特别容易受到需求突变与算法中介的冲击。平台劳动是最清晰的例子：算法分配任务、设定价格、监控绩效并施加惩罚，而工人可能被归类为独立承包人，使平台得以外部化风险、逃避社会保护缴费。

“AI 提高生产率，生产率最终提高工资”的传导并非自动。它取决于劳资议价力量、产品市场竞争程度，以及数据与知识产权的规则。当少数公司控制基础模型、算力与分发渠道时，AI 收益可能不成比例地流向资本；工人体验到的可能是强化而非解放：更多监控、更快节奏、更少自主权。

公正转型的政策议程应包括：把社会保护与标准雇佣关系脱钩，建立全民或近全民的儿童、健康与收入支持；赋予工人对算法管理的知情、解释与申诉权；把技能政策从短期训练营转向基础素养、数字判断与持续学习能力；把集体谈判与社会对话纳入技术治理。AI 供应链跨越国界，劳动保护却仍以国家为单位，平台可以选择规则最弱的司法辖区，形成逐底竞争。国际社会应推动算法透明的最低标准、平台工人的跨境社会保障协调，以及数字活动税收的公平分享。SDG 8 不只是岗位数量，而是工作能否让人有尊严、有声音、分享生产率收益。
